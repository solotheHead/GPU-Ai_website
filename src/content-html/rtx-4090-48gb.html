RTX 4090 48GB for AI: specifications, price and buying in the EU

The RTX 4090 48GB is a modified GeForce RTX 4090 with twice the standard memory and a dual-slot blower cooler. It gives local LLM inference and multi-GPU servers 48GB of VRAM per card for far less than a professional 48GB or 96GB GPU. This guide explains what the card is, where it fits and what to check before you buy.

What is the RTX 4090 48GB?

NVIDIA sells the GeForce RTX 4090 with 24GB of GDDR6X memory. The 48GB version is not an NVIDIA retail product. Specialist manufacturers move the RTX 4090 graphics processor (AD102) onto a custom circuit board with memory chips on both sides — a layout similar to the RTX 3090 — and fit a blower cooler in a dual-slot, server-friendly shape.

The card keeps the RTX 4090’s compute engine and 384-bit memory bus, but with 48GB of VRAM. For AI work, that memory often matters more than raw speed: it decides which models fit on one card and how much context you can serve.

What 48GB of VRAM changes for AI

Large language models usually run out of memory before they run out of compute. As a rule of thumb, model weights need about 2 bytes per parameter at 16-bit precision, 1 byte at 8-bit and about 0.5 bytes at 4-bit, plus memory for the context (KV cache) and the runtime.

  • Models around 70B parameters at 4-bit need roughly 35–40GB for the weights. They fit on one 48GB card with room for a moderate context window, while 24GB cards have to split them across two GPUs.
  • Models in the 30B class at 8-bit fit on a single card with room for longer contexts or several parallel users.
  • LoRA and QLoRA fine-tuning, image and video generation and 3D rendering also benefit from the extra headroom.

Two cards give 96GB in total — as much memory as one RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell — for about two-thirds of its indicative price. The trade-offs are slower communication between GPUs than within one card and a higher combined power draw (up to 900W against 600W).

RTX 3090 48GB: the lower-cost alternative

The RTX 3090 48GB applies the same idea to the previous-generation GA102 processor: 10,496 CUDA cores, 48GB of GDDR6X on a 384-bit bus and about 936 GB/s of memory bandwidth, in a dual-slot blower design. It is slower than the RTX 4090 48GB in compute-heavy work. In memory-bound LLM inference the difference is smaller, because the two cards have similar memory bandwidth. At $2,000 indicative, it is the lowest-cost route to 48GB per card in our range.

Buying a 48GB card: what to check

Most 48GB RTX cards offered in Europe are sold through marketplaces and import listings. Whoever you buy from, check:

  • Who is responsible if the card fails. NVIDIA does not provide a warranty for modified cards. The seller’s commercial warranty and your legal rights against the seller are what protect you.
  • Invoice, VAT and import costs. Cards shipped from outside the EU can add import VAT, customs handling and delays to the listed price.
  • Testing before dispatch. Ask which stress and memory tests were run, and whether the card was tested in a configuration like yours.
  • Power and airflow. At up to 450W per card, several cards need suitable power supplies, connectors and strong front-to-back airflow.
  • Noise. Blower coolers are loud under full load. They suit a server room or colocation better than an office.
  • Software. These cards are generally reported to work with standard NVIDIA drivers, but confirm compatibility with your driver, CUDA and framework versions.

Frequently asked questions

Is the RTX 4090 48GB an official NVIDIA product?

No. NVIDIA’s retail RTX 4090 has 24GB. The 48GB card is a third-party modification of the RTX 4090 design. The NVIDIA name identifies the underlying technology; it does not mean that NVIDIA authorised the modification or provides a warranty for it.

Can I use it for gaming?

It can run games, but it is built for compute. Reviews of 48GB cards report gaming results below a standard RTX 4090 and a loud blower cooler. For gaming, a standard retail card is the better choice.
